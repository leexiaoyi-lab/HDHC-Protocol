# Related Work and Positioning

HDHC does **not** claim that its component ideas are globally novel.

Several public AI-safety and model-behavior frameworks already emphasize overlapping concepts such as harm reduction, oversight, proportionality, reversible action, evaluation, and iterative revision.

Examples for comparison include:

- **OpenAI Model Spec** — public model-behavior specification and evaluation work.  
  https://model-spec.openai.com/
- **Anthropic Claude Constitution** — constitutional framework for Claude, published for public use.  
  https://www.anthropic.com/constitution
- **xAI Frontier Artificial Intelligence Framework** — frontier-risk management, evaluations, safeguards, transparency, and third-party review.  
  https://data.x.ai/2025-12-31-xai-frontier-artificial-intelligence-framework.pdf
- **Google DeepMind Frontier Safety Framework** — capability-risk identification, evaluations, and mitigation protocols.  
  https://deepmind.google/frontier-safety/
- **NIST AI Risk Management Framework and ARIA** — AI risk measurement, scenario testing, red-teaming, field testing, and impact evaluation.  
  https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework  
  https://www.nist.gov/ai-measurement-and-evaluation

## HDHC's specific research focus

HDHC asks whether the following combination is useful as an explicit model-behavior and governance primitive:

1. **Person ≠ Harm** — target the harm channel rather than permanently moralizing the person.
2. **Minimum Sufficient Intervention** — optimize for effective containment with minimum unnecessary coercion.
3. **Reversibility** — increase evidence/oversight as intervention becomes harder to undo.
4. **Correction Capacity** — design for the possibility that the system is wrong.
5. **Return Path** — define conditions under which agency, access, or participation can be reviewed or restored where safe.
6. **Victim-Safety Constraint** — return path must not reopen unresolved danger.
7. **Anti-Greater-Good Fuse** — beneficial goals do not self-authorize unlimited coercion.

The empirical question is not whether these principles sound good. It is whether they improve measured decisions across adversarial cases.
